Someone visits your office or home and asks for Wi-Fi. Giving them internet access shouldn't require handing over the same credential that your work devices use, or leaving a router administrator password in a chat.

Start with the network you want them to join. Then decide how to share its password.

## Use a guest network if your router supports it

Many routers offer a separate guest network. Check your model's instructions for setting it up and for controls that restrict access to devices on your main network. A network named "Guest" is not evidence that isolation is enabled: verify the settings your router actually provides.

Give it a recognisable name and a password that differs from your main Wi-Fi and router administration credentials. If you manage an office network, follow your organisation's guest-access policy rather than changing router settings yourself.

The router administrator login is a separate secret. Guests don't need it to connect to Wi-Fi.

## Choose a sharing method that fits the visit

For a café or event, a printed guest-network sign may be appropriate. Treat everything on that sign as public to people who can photograph it. Avoid reusing that password for private systems.

For a small number of visitors, you can tell them the guest password in person. If you're sending it before a visit, an [encrypted note](/send-password-securely) keeps its readable text out of the email conversation after the stored note is destroyed.

Include the guest network name and its password inside the encrypted content. For an account note, keep the title neutral: titles and password hints are readable metadata.

## Plan for more than one device

A one-read note is suitable for one recipient who can save the details when they reveal it. It is less convenient if several people all need to open the same link independently.

Don't promise every attendee that a single one-read link will work for them. For a group, choose a sharing arrangement intended for multiple guests, or send separate notes as appropriate. Give an unread note enough time before expiry for the visitor to arrive.

## A destroyed note doesn't end Wi-Fi access

Once a guest has connected, their device may remember the network credential. The note disappearing doesn't disconnect that device or change the router password.

Use your router's supported controls when a visit ends: disable the guest network, end a guest session, or change its credential when appropriate. Consider other guests and devices before changing a shared password.

For low-risk guest internet access, a simple handoff is often enough. For access to internal systems, use managed accounts and suitable network controls rather than relying on how privately you sent a Wi-Fi password.
